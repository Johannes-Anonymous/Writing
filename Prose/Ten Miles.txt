# Ten Miles

He was always running ten miles. “Wheel of Fortune” would be playing for fifteen minutes, and then he'd be off that beat-up couch and stomping his feet in the closet, hustling to get them into a pair of shoes and his body in a jacket before the clock ticked any farther.

I ran with him once, back in July, under the bare sun along the river. I wasn't in great shape, and I was surprised that I could barely hear my locomotive breaths over the flopping of his shoes, his loose soles smacking the pavement like dying white fish with every stroke.

The day after he died, I went back to the trail.

There were stains on the pavement, and flowers blossoming in the grass. Someone had set up a picture and his name against a road sign.

The switchback above wasn't roped off, but the police were still there, on and off, interviewing the passing runners, bikers, and hikers, hoping that someone had seen or heard anything that would piece a story together.

The sun was almost setting, just past the last phase of what one would truly call daylight, and I was anxious, without being able to name why. Dylan's parents had come to the apartment the night before, flying in from California immediately after hearing from the police. They mostly just stood around and looked, seeing for the first time where their son was living that year. Being so far away, they had seldom visited.

My other roommate and I gathered Dylan's possessions from the common areas together for them: shoes, jackets, his toothbrush, and other such things. They said we should keep his dishes and furniture.

I think often about the day he died, and I remember it well. Late that afternoon, I woke up from a deep nap on his old, beat-up couch. I opened my eyes to our roommate standing at my feet, straight-backed and stone-faced. Our windows were open and I could smell the autumn air, though there was barely a breeze. The trees behind Tommy were a fiery and glorious red, and he looked at me without emotion and said in one word, “Dylan'sDead.” Then he marched into the bathroom, slammed the door, and started to retch.

We didn't speak to each other much for the next week, at least not around the apartment. Things weren't tense, but the air just felt strange. Tommy spent more time staring out the window, and I spent more time with old video games, going back to originals from my childhood.

I don't know if Dylan remembered it, but he and I first met long before college, when we were still kids. I didn't remember it myself until just before his death, and didn't get around to asking him about it. It happened when our elementary classes, among hundreds of others, were both in Chicago on an extracurricular summer field trip, celebrating the inauguration of some massive new museums and exhibits on one of our nation's anniversaries. My class was having lunch in a spacious white hall, our teacher and chaperones sitting with us and shepherding us through the line and to our tables.

On my way to my seat, another kid with a goofy smile and a shock of sun-blond hair walked past me with his tray, bouncing through his steps. With a natural ease and without a break in his stride, he hopped on the low bench at our table and kept walking. He reached the end of our class, where I was meant to sit, and plopped himself down and quickly stuck the straw into his juice box. He was grinning like a merry thief at the rest of us, who had no idea who he was. Yet, he was immediately at home among us, a happy stranger in a new land. Instantly, we were all his friends.

A few minutes later, his frantic teacher finally found him, exclaiming, “Dylan! Where have you been?” and pulled him right off the bench. His smile and laughter only grew brighter as he let himself be led by the hands on his shoulders in the direction of his own classmates, tossing his unopened box of Animal Crackers to one of my friends. He was bouncing like a rubber ball as he skipped away from us and his teacher, and it was this same cheery gait that I eventually recognized years later.

His funeral was held back in California, but they did have a visitation and short memorial service on campus, since he had made so many friends. That weekend was surreal in many ways, and a permanent disruption to our typical routine of pancakes and new episodes of Top Gear in our pajamas.

I met a lot more of his friends at that service, which was not surprising: I always met new people when walking with Dylan between classes. Some of them I felt like I already knew, once I found out a little bit about them, since Dylan was always sharing the good news about his friends and acquaintances.

I was on the fence about going out west for the funeral. It would have been impractical for me—interrupting my classes and costing me a fair bit of my summer's savings—but I couldn't deny the tug I was feeling toward the religious aspect. Dylan had referred to his family and himself as “High Anglicans,” and though I barely knew what he meant, I had seen and heard enough to know that they had a more formal and elaborate church service, and took it seriously.

Growing up, my siblings and I were usually pulled to church by our parents, and while my only negative memories are ones of boredom, I didn't particularly appreciate or enjoy it. It was only slightly uncomfortable to fall out of the habit of attending during my freshman year — I was simply more attracted to girls, late Saturday nights, and sleeping in.

But some old embers were being kindled within me, and in places and ways that I couldn't name. I wanted something I had never known or experienced before, some way of sending my heartache for Dylan off to wherever his spirit went, and I didn't know how to find this. I just had the subtle sense of being pulled back to a church.

I felt anxious and conflicted about this until the moment that Dylan's parents left us for the airport, and then I knew that I'd be staying back. Wherever Dylan was, it was beyond this earth, and following his remains to California wasn't going to bring me any closer to him.

Months passed, bringing winter and then taking it away. Between the festive autumn colors and the green buds and thick mud of spring, we had deep snow, bone-shattering cold, and black, frozen nights. Navigating the streets and tunnels of campus in the long dark hours of winter, I felt like a heavy cloak of depression was being slowly draped upon my shoulders.

We had an optional “J term,” which was either a trip or a few weeks of condensed and intensive coursework in the time between New Year's and the spring semester. I chose to stay home, hoping that the extended time with family and familiarity would lighten my grief and brighten my spirits. It did, but the change didn't last, and my return to campus brought back my biting fears, which though sharp, remained vague and unresolved.

I missed Dylan, and I missed something more than him. The air was thinner and the sunshine paler without his laugh, jokes, and updates on all that he encountered around campus.

By the time we went home for the summer, the police still hadn't determined what happened. It seemed obvious that he had fallen from the switchback above, but no one knew how or why. An accidental slip didn't account for the fatal impact, and both suicide and vicious intent seemed outrageously implausible.

Tommy and I had been interviewed multiple times, as the case officer would continually circle back after learning of another facet of Dylan's life from his wide circle of friends. Dylan knew many people, but Tommy and I were the ones he chose to live with, and it seemed that the officer thought us to be closer to the center of his life, and therefore a potential answer, than either Tommy or I did.

Two weeks before finals, Tommy surprised me by asking if he could spend summer break with me, in my hometown. Many ambitious students like him were looking for internships or similar experiences related to their field after our sophomore year, and we both knew that there would be only general, low-skill jobs back at my home. I was going back for the comfort of my family, and perhaps Tommy was looking for something similar.

My siblings were wonderful to be around for those few months of sunshine and warmth. They're all younger, and I shared much of the summer with my two oldest brothers, the three of us and Tommy being on a rotation of jobs for the city's Parks and Recreation department.

Often, Tommy and I ended up working the pool together. One would run the snack bar, the other lifeguard, and a third perform miscellaneous cleaning duties and cover for others on their breaks. Save for the diligence required while lifeguarding, it was laidback and easy work, and I enjoyed the sunshine and reprieve from our rigorous classes.

After the first few weeks, Tommy began taking an interest in one of our coworkers, Natalie. I began seeing them together more and more often, and they would often spend their work breaks together. Eventually they were going on evening dates, and Tommy even asked my parents if she could join us for a family dinner, which they were delighted to agree to. Tommy helped plan and prepare the smoked brisket and side dishes, showing off cooking skills that we didn't know he had.

I enjoyed seeing the two of them together, and getting to know Natalie better. She had been a grade below me growing up, and our friend groups didn't directly overlap. I knew a fair bit about her and was familiar with her personality already, though; in our small town, there were no strangers.

As the summer started to wind down, Tommy began withdrawing into his shell again, and I wondered if their friendship would change or continue to grow. Natalie had stayed home after graduating, and was now considering starting classes at our college, to be closer to him.

A few times, I tried talking with him about this, during our occasional, though regular, nights off and alone together. We'd be upstairs in his room, at least one of us playing a game on the TV, or shooting hoops together on the crooked slabs by the alley, and I'd broach the issue of his emotional withdrawal against Natalie's continued interest. However, my engagements only seems to foster a deeper pattern of silence and solitude on his part.

Soon, she was the one planning their dates, and he called her less and less. I don't know what kind of conversations they did or didn't have, but when they were alone together on a work break or keeping to themselves around the house, their voices were softer and expressions subdued.

Natalie took all of this as writing on the wall, and broke off their relationship a week before classes resumed. She tried to be gentle about it, and shared that she'd be sticking around town for the foreseeable future. She had no great reason for the financial investment of college and the time away from her family and artistic pursuits if Tommy wasn't going to take a more active interest in their relationship.

Tommy came home angry that night, a state in which I'd never seen him before. He only said a few words, but they were strong and forceful, and his whiplash manners broadcast his feelings clearly.

I tried staying away from him for a while, but he was hard to avoid between work and the limited square footage at home. Finally, I told him to quit his bitter and self-righteous sulking and either forget about her, or to go back, apologize, and make it up with her. I knew that she'd at least be willing to consider seeing him again.

And so, on the weekend we were packing up for school, in the few dozen hours we had between our last work shift and the start of classes, he gave her a call, went over to see her, and came back with just a few words. “Friends,” he said, “just friends. But she wants to see me again soon, even before the fall.” It was definitely still summer, but clearly near its end.
