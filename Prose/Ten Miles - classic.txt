# Ten Miles (classic)

## Originally began in the fall semester of 2012, as part of my 1101W Creative Writing class at the University of Minnesota.

He was always running ten miles. “Wheel of Fortune” would be playing for 15 minutes, and then he’d be off that beat-up couch and stomping his feet in the closet, hustling to get his feet into a pair of shoes and his body in a jacket before the clocked ticked further.

I ran with him once, back in July, under the bare sun along the river. I wasn’t in great shape, and I was surprised that I could hardly hear my locomotive breathes over the flopping of his shoes, smacking the pavement with every stride. Turning my head, I saw the white soles shake like dying fish as they escaped the ground for one brief moment after another.

The day after it happened, I went back to the trail.

—

There weren’t any eyewitness reports of the fall, but I believe that my mind briefly left my body that early-evening and saw what happened. I woke up from a somber nap on the couch to see my other roommate standing by my feet, the tree behind him thinning out its auburn leaves. He said in one word, ‘Dylan’sdead”, and then walked straight into the bathroom, slamming the door.

As I fell asleep that night, I saw Dylan making the last turn by the stoplight, noticing the rising moon at the moment his right shoe falls apart, the fabric completely splitting at the toes and his bare foot sliding through. He is pulled forward by inertia, and ends up on the sidewalk with both hands scraped, feeling them go hot as he looks up and sees the eastern sky, purple.

From there, my vision goes blank, and I hear many sounds: The jet wash of a plane, bright chirping from a cloud of birds shooting through the air, the crash of a waterfall jumbled with the unnatural screech of a car horn. Then my vision is back, and Dylan is falling down the cliff past the other side of the stone railing, hitting many of the rocks on the way down. He ends back-up, with one of the small streams from above splashing between his shoulders. He lays ten feet away from the trail and thirty seconds away from the jogging couple, not yet around the bend.

We were planning on seeing a band from his high school in early November, but of course that didn’t happen. Many other things happened, and I remember them all, held in the glare of varnished wood, the smell of incense, and the gentle slap of leaves blowing in the wind.

—

Dylan and I grew up in nearby towns. We met in high school before senior year, through competing sport teams. He ran cross-country and wrestled, while I wrestled and played in the marching band. When I earned my driver’s license late, his friend John’s house was one of places I frequented. The three of us used to wear our dirtiest pairs of shoes and go to the movies, just to see if could pick up dates for the night in them. During our first week at college, Dylan and I did the same thing in a brand new town.

It wasn’t long before we found each other different, as we changed throughout the semesters. Still, he had a way to cut through the unnecessary parts of my personality. There was something jagged in the words he spoke to me that final morning - “Watch the corners,” lightly popping my shoulder with his left hand and walking out of the room to his early class. I was washing dinner plates, trying not to break any.

—

I went to the trail the next day, getting up before my roommates, and put weight against the caution tape. It’s beyond me if I saw any blood. I called my parents that morning, and felt my back ratchet and straighten as I stood on the curb and they pulled up to take me home. The other roommates and I took a week off from class.

At home, time just disappeared. I was without any short-term goals for a while: no tests, no work shifts, nothing in my hands for my mind to hold on to.

I saw a few hometown friends; most were now attending college, others involved in the Navy and Marines. We spent our first night driving on a country road out of town. Someone put the heater on full, and all of us put our windows down. The car was being driven straight at the moon, with the red lights of the dashboard glowing upon us.

When I think about that night, I know I wanted to lean back in the seat and sleep until I was young again. I’ll remember that feeling until the day I die; I haven’t had it since. Hovering over the road, the car was stuffy with silence and a shaky pride - so far, all of us had shown ourselves to be capable of carrying on, capable of bearing a weight unasked for. It was clear to me that we still in shallow water though; more weight would come in time when the next one of us passed. We saw ourselves in each other; these were the friends I would spend entire afternoons with biking through town.

Now we had classes, and some of us had short stints of full-responsibility. I had tried harder than some, spending the last two summers working graveyard shifts at warehouse and factory jobs. Feeling the calluses on my hands as we passed a sign for Interstate 90, I felt the car grow hot and close in on me. I looked to Matt, and shuddered slightly. There was nothing in his eyes that I could name.

We stopped driving after two hours, and walked out into a midnight web of stars and moonlight. Someone picked up a rock and threw it into a nearby cornfield.

—

Eighty miles south and a few days later, the wake smelled like wax. I remember stepping from the car, carrying a package of mementos for a display table, and feeling that I was about to stumble. Inside the funeral home, the conversation between friends and family was stiff, as if everyone was propped up on stilts. Conversation with some of my peers was difficult that night. I was on a moving train, watching them shrink and disappear with the station.

Others must have noticed what was going on as well; I saw tectonic plates shifting in my friends, and felt words slide between us and disappear. Somehow, my family managed to stay in the funeral home for five hours, during which time I found relief by guzzling apple juice in the back room.

Standing by a bouquet of flowers before the sun went down, I watched people shuffle past the casket. I’m not sure what they were thinking, but I wanted to pull Dylan out of the casket, find his driver’s license in a pocket, and shout his full name into his face. After that, I wanted to clench the tie that was around his throat and shake him until he moved his mouth to speak. My forehead grew hot as I eyed the line of people that snaked around the room.

—

The night went by terribly slow. I was thinking about the four of us who would spend summers together, wasting time we knew we would lose. Now, three of us stood in front of Dylan’s casket, breathing in unison, staring at our future, our friend’s body.

“He shaved,” John said quietly.

Matt nudged me in the ribs and replied, “Nah, probably was the mortician.”

I slowly walked away.

Near the end of the night, my mother was standing with Dylan’s mom, the two of them posed by the entrance. I could see the wind calmly sweep their hair as the cold came in from the blue November night. Across the street, someone was carrying a lit torch between houses, and shafts of light snuck out between swing-sets in the backyards. I dug my fingers into my pockets to pull out a grocery list, a pack of gum, and a lottery ticket from a week before. It would be another hour before we would leave and I could sleep in my bed, to dream of all the weights capable of changing me.

—

The funeral and burial were on a Saturday. My dad drove us to the graveyard in silence. Winter had dropped its hammer the day before, and snow covered everything. The trees had a few leaves left, and they wavered above the cars huffing fumes and spewing gravel. My mother offered me the front seat as we were picked up in front of the church. I opened the door while removing my hat, letting November bite into my ears for a brief moment.

The crowd huddled around the snowy plot, near the back-end of the cemetery. Black coats were all around, and a few people were stomping their feet in the cold. The priest, tall and mean-faced, looked down and opened his Bible. A train whistle suddenly blew from the other side of the river, and he waited before opening his mouth in a raucous voice.

For the short time there, I kept my hands in my coat pockets, my eyes on Dylan’s mother, and my feet planted. She wore a red flower, parting her hair above the ear. Her first son, quite a few years older than Dylan, stood right next to her, their shoulders rubbing. Before the casket was lowered, I saw Dylan’s brother look over to his father, and I swear I felt something pull away from both of them, silently disappearing after it lodged itself in their throats. Their family was so small and wound-up that morning; I prayed for them, and the next time they would find themselves at their kitchen table.

Abruptly, the wind blew; there was something in the air, reaching from the half-lowered casket, and it pulled the breath from my lungs. I imagined Dylan standing beside me, looking me in the eye. I knew that if he were here, he wouldn’t say a thing, and I wouldn’t ask any questions. Whatever was coming, I would wait for it.

As we left, my parents hugged me tight. I turned my face to see the intersecting branches of the trees. They were dark and dirty against the snowy sky, open to the world around us. I thought of the sea of roots that grew below us, patiently digging into warm earth. Facing the dispersing crowd for a moment, my eyes met with John. He walked over, grabbed my arm, and breathed like a horse while staring into my face. My muscles tensed, feeling a darkness radiate between us, and I held out a minute before gently pushing him away.









## Scratch

Between the wake and the funeral, I saw John one more time. Whenever he would go somewhere, he would never ring the bell, choosing instead to knock. It was as if your afternoon was guaranteed to bust open when he came around.

John gave the door a good shaking. The mailman had not come around the neighborhood yet, and I was hanging around in a t-shirt, without any plans. He said we should take a walk, go downtown.

“They tore down the theatre,” he said.

“Umbrella?” I said. It was starting to drizzle.

His reply was quick, “Yeah right.”

He was wearing a dirty pair of shoes. I looked for mine in the closet, an old special pair to match his, and couldn’t find them. I thought of asking my mother if she had seen them, but John was holding the door open with his foot. I grabbed my dad’s tennis shoes and we went out.

—

There were flowers hanging from the light-posts, dripping water onto the street. We took our time walking to the main block of the city. It was downhill the entire way. John spoke up when we passed Washington school.

“You know something funny? I broke a kid’s arm on that playground, on his birthday. I shoved him right off one of the slides.”

I looked at him, to see where his eyes were pointing. He turned from the school to face me.

“His nose bled like nothing else. The sucker’s elbow went straight into his face.”

I nodded.

“You ever done something like that?” he asked. I again nodded slowly, and told him of the time in junior high I punched a black kid in the gut, just to know what it would feel like.

We crossed the street.

“Well, I shouldn’t be surprised,” he said. “There’s a crooked line in all of us.”

I was looking right at him when he said this, and with those words, the glass between us broke. From there, I wanted to walk home on my own.
